About lab and how we work

Public professional and research overview prepared on 4 October 2026. Personal health history and private laboratory records are excluded. This is background for an English two-host discussion, not a screenplay. Bracketed references identify the source notes below. Future scenarios are possibilities, not promises or completed work.

The idea became clear during a walk in the forest. Pawel Kuklik was listening to a podcast about autonomous AI agents and began to imagine a different way to organize a laboratory. He already had scientific questions he wanted to pursue. The obstacle was finding the time to supervise every transition: from an idea to a search, from a paper to a dataset, from a calculation to a result worth trusting. Could an AI system carry more of that work forward while he continued his professional research? [L1]

There is an earlier forest in this story. As a child with a microscope, Pawel collected insects and plants from the garden and woods, drawn to the complexity revealed through the lens. He later studied physics. During his fourth year, a department studying the dynamics of the heart gave that curiosity a direction. His master's work involved simulations of cardiac electrical activity, leading to collaboration with physicians and a career in cardiac electrophysiology. His public biography describes consulting for a startup developing an atrial fibrillation mapping system. The recurring interest is the meeting of living systems, measurement and computation. [L1]

The laboratory focuses on axial spondyloarthritis, with research directions spanning disease mechanisms, interventions and everyday practices, structural progression, and prospects for repair. These are connected questions, but their answers require different evidence. The question behind its founding is whether a small computational laboratory could sustain useful, inspectable work while its founder continued his professional research. Curiosity supplies persistence. It cannot determine whether a hypothesis is true. The lab's public mission makes that tension central: ambition establishes the direction; evidence limits the conclusion. [L1–L3]

The organization is unusual. SpA Lab has one human founder supported by AI tools. Pawel sets broad direction. An AI coordinator, identified publicly as GPT-6 Astra operating through Codex and connected tools, plans work, assigns bounded tasks, integrates outputs and reports progress. “Director” describes an operational role within this project. It is not a professional credential, a claim of human judgment, or a sign of endorsement by the model's developer. Specialist collaborators are sought for clinical interpretation, methodological criticism and future experimental validation. Those relationships are an ambition, not a completed institute hidden behind the website. [L1–L3]

Autonomy here is a practical question about how much work can proceed within an agreed scope. The system may carry a question through literature retrieval, extraction, code and an explanation without Pawel manually directing every step. Yet it works in sessions. Saved protocols, evidence tables, code and decision records provide continuity. A future session resumes from those records; there is no continuously awake researcher inside the computer. Scheduled work depends on the local computer and application being available. Reliable unattended completion remains under development. [L1,L2]

This makes the laboratory itself an experiment in organization. Its possible value is easy to understand: scientific work contains many useful but laborious transitions. A relevant paper must be found, its population understood, a result located, an uncertain denominator noticed, and its relationship to other reports preserved. AI may help connect these stages and make modest projects feasible for a small team. Whether it actually saves time or improves reliability must be judged from its outputs, corrections and operating record. Generating more text is not, by itself, progress.

The research loop starts by making a question small enough to answer. What population is being studied? Compared with what? Which outcome, measured when? What observation would count against the proposed explanation? Writing those choices before interpreting results helps expose the difference between investigating an idea and searching for support for a preferred answer. The plan can change when reality requires it, but the change should remain visible. Otherwise an apparently successful prediction may have been invented after the result was known. [L2]

Retrieval then becomes a chain of evidence rather than a collection of impressive titles. Several papers may describe the same participants. An abstract can omit crucial methods. An inaccessible supplement remains inaccessible. A missing value must stay unknown instead of becoming a convenient assumption. Recording the source and its access level lets a later reader see the difference between a fully inspected article and a provisional interpretation of a summary. The purpose is to preserve the reasoning path, including the points where it becomes thin. [L2,L4]

When calculation is useful, the lab keeps the inputs and code so the arithmetic can be rerun. A separate AI task can challenge pivotal extractions and interpretations. That is useful error checking, with a serious limitation: related models can share habits, blind spots and confident mistakes. A second AI agreeing with the first does not turn an interpretation into independent scientific validation. A reproducible program can also reproduce an incorrect assumption perfectly. External expertise and evidence from the world remain necessary. [L1,L2,L4]

The first completed public pilot shows why these apparently ordinary details can matter. Its question was: what does “non-progression” mean in accessible reports of spinal structural outcomes? This sounds almost too modest for an AI laboratory. Yet it exposes a trap in the way research travels from tables to headlines. When readers hear that damage did not progress, they may picture a completely unchanged spine. The statistical definition used in a study can mean something narrower. [L4]

The pilot, called P002, assembled nine parent trial families and 46 endpoint records. Four families supported five matched comparison rows. A family groups reports belonging to the same underlying trial, so an extension paper does not automatically become a new independent experiment. The audit preserved exact thresholds, populations, imaging details, missing information and source locations. It was a bounded descriptive project, not a comprehensive systematic review, a comparison ranking medicines, or a cure-discovery study. [L4]

Here is the concrete example. In the MEASURE-1 comparative reading dataset, 60.7 percent were classified as non-progressors when the change in a radiographic spinal score, called mSASSS, had to be zero or less. Allowing a change of up to two points produced 82.1 percent. Subtracting those rounded published rates gives 21.4 percentage points. The participants did not become biologically different when the classification rule changed. The boundary changed. This example concerns the same cohort under two definitions, not the benefit of one treatment over another. The original data come from Braun and colleagues' 2019 report; the lab's contribution is the scoped audit and interpretation. [L4,S4]

That distinction deserves a moment. A threshold compresses a range of measurements into a category. Compression can be useful, but the rule becomes part of the result. A stricter cutoff and a more permissive cutoff answer different questions. Even “less than two” and “two or less” are not interchangeable. The lab therefore kept its comparisons separate, did not pool them into one effect, and did not present their size as clinical importance. Some underlying sources and reading exercises also overlap, which limits how independently the examples can corroborate one another. [L4]

The deeper lesson is the distinction between inflammation, progression and repair. Reduced inflammatory activity is one outcome. Preventing additional structural change over follow-up is another. Demonstrating that an existing structural lesion has regressed is a third. Feeling better is also valuable, but symptoms cannot silently stand in for an imaging outcome. To investigate repair, researchers would need an appropriate, predefined way to follow the same existing lesion and distinguish biological change from measurement variation. The pilot identified no lesion-specific repair endpoint in the inspected structural material for its nine families. That finding is limited to that material; it does not establish that repair is impossible or has never been studied. [L2,L4]

This is what a useful early AI research output can look like: a clearer question, a traceable dataset and a claim made more precise. The report exposes its limitations, including restricted searching, accessible-source selection, incomplete supplementary and historical checks, and internal rather than external review. A reader can inspect the public records and reproduce the descriptive arithmetic. Reproducing those differences does not redo the literature search or certify every interpretation. The distinction is part of the result, not an embarrassment to remove from the story. [L4]

The broader scientific landscape offers reasons for interest in what AI might eventually add. Three published examples illustrate different pieces of the puzzle. They are achievements by other research groups, not capabilities demonstrated by SpA Lab. They also come from different years and solve different problems; they should not be presented as a single system that can autonomously do all science.

In “Highly accurate protein structure prediction with AlphaFold,” published in 2021, Jumper and colleagues reported a system that predicted protein structures with accuracy approaching experimental structures for many targets at CASP14, a blind protein-structure prediction assessment. This is a striking computational achievement. It can help researchers form structural hypotheses, but a predicted structure alone does not establish a molecule's effect in a living person, the safety of a treatment, or a route to repairing spinal damage. AlphaFold illustrates progress on an important scientific subproblem, not a complete autonomous discovery pipeline. [S1]

“A mobile robotic chemist,” published in 2020 by Burger and colleagues, demonstrated a robot conducting and selecting experiments in a working chemistry laboratory. In the reported campaign, it performed 688 experiments over eight days while exploring mixtures for photocatalytic hydrogen production. The abstract reports a sixfold improvement in activity over the initial formulations. The task, materials and laboratory environment were defined by researchers. This demonstrates a physical experimental search in a bounded setting, not general biological reasoning or a medical result. The accessible publisher abstract supports these facts; the full article was not inspected for this brief. [S2]

In “Autonomous chemical research with large language models,” published in 2023, Boiko and colleagues described Coscientist, a system connecting language models with search, code execution and laboratory tools. Their demonstrations included planning and executing established chemical reactions. Laboratory resources and tasks were supplied by people, and some physical handling remained manual. These examples support a narrower claim than the title might suggest: parts of a defined chemical workflow can be coordinated through such a system. They do not establish a general scientist able to select important questions, safely run any experiment, or validate a therapy without people. [S3]

Taken together, these demonstrations suggest several building blocks: computational prediction, algorithm-guided physical experimentation, and software that connects information to instruments. Combining building blocks is not automatic. Each arrives with assumptions about its inputs, task boundaries and available equipment. A model may propose an experiment that an instrument cannot perform. An instrument may produce measurements that do not test the biological question. A polished explanation may hide both failures. The difficult work is making the connection scientifically meaningful.

For SpA Lab, a plausible next stage is still largely theoretical and computational. If a worthwhile question and suitable data become available, AI could help compare mechanistic explanations, map evidence to explicit hypotheses, or test how strongly a conclusion depends on an analytical choice. A useful simulation would need assumptions stated clearly and predictions connected to measurable quantities. A public-data analysis would need appropriate provenance, permissions, phenotype definitions and checks for bias. These are possible future workflows, not claims that the lab has already performed them or discovered a new mechanism.

One attractive role is helping decide which uncertainty should be resolved next. Suppose two plausible explanations fit the same observation. A computational analysis might reveal a measurement on which their predictions differ. The value would lie in producing a feasible discriminating test, including an outcome that could defeat the preferred explanation. The work could also end with a negative result: available data cannot separate the alternatives. That can still help a future collaborator avoid an uninformative experiment. This is an illustrative scenario, not a current SpA Lab finding.

Wet-lab research would require a further step. If qualified collaborators, suitable facilities, resources and approvals were available, AI might assist with experimental planning and analysis, while validated automation could perform selected procedures. Human specialists would need to judge whether an assay actually represents the biological question and whether its controls are adequate. Independent replication would still matter. More experiments per day would be valuable only if the measurements were trustworthy and the experiments informative. Nothing in the current lab record establishes an operating robotic biology laboratory, a validated disease model, or an experimentally proven new treatment.

The risks grow with the ambition. Retrieval can miss contrary evidence. A model can invent a citation, merge distinct patient groups, or turn an association into a causal story. Code can calculate the wrong quantity with impeccable precision. Automation can spread an error through many files before anyone notices. Biological systems introduce further uncertainty when a promising result in one experimental setting fails in another. Traceability, bounded tasks, preserved unknowns, corrective review and qualified collaboration reduce opportunities for these failures; they do not guarantee their elimination. [L1,L2]

Privacy and data rights belong in that assessment. Public availability does not automatically permit every reuse. The lab separates private working records from public reports; this episode uses a deliberately public-safe source. Locally saved files do not mean an AI workflow is entirely offline. Continued operation also depends on tools, service access and changing software behavior, so a working demonstration should not become a permanent reliability promise. [L2]

There is also a communication challenge. A compelling conversation can make an uncertain result feel settled. A phrase like “AI discovered” can collapse a long chain of human choices, data, software and checking into a misleading character in a story. The podcast therefore needs to keep asking what was actually done, by whom, and what observation supports the claim. Listeners should be able to distinguish the founder's motivation, the lab's completed work, achievements elsewhere, and conditional ideas for the future.

The hopeful possibility is that a small team can do more useful, inspectable work by delegating some of research's coordination and repetition. For SpA Lab, the immediate test is whether that arrangement produces questions, records and analyses that other people can understand and challenge. Its longer ambition is to contribute to understanding axial spondyloarthritis and, through appropriate evidence and collaboration, help make better interventions possible. There is no promised cure or timetable. The thread connecting the childhood microscope, the forest walk and the current laboratory is a wish to look more closely at living systems—and to make each claim answerable to what was actually seen.
