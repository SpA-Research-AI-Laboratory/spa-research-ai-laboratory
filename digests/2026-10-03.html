01 / CEO report

A useful first step—and a delivery failure to fix

After completing our first project on how studies measure changes in the spine, the lab is turning to another of its founding questions: what can dietary changes do for people with axial spondyloarthritis? This is the form of SpA that concerns the spine and sacroiliac joints, where the spine connects to the pelvis. A study may test an entire eating pattern, a supplement, or a programme that combines diet with exercise. It may measure symptoms, an immune response, or something else. These are different experiments, even when they appear under the same heading of “diet and SpA.”

Today we completed the rules for a focused review. This written plan, called a protocol, specifies which people, interventions and measurements we will examine. We also checked several original reports. Those checks exposed practical problems that the review must handle: conflicting descriptions of how a study was conducted, comparisons that are easy to misread, and multiple papers about the same participants.

The next useful output will be an organized account of the available studies: what each tested, whom it included, what it measured, and how much of the original report we could inspect. A review of diet and SpA already exists, so another broad summary alone would add little. Our possible contribution is to make the underlying evidence easier to judge. We have not yet established how much this will add to existing work. The earlier review is listed below.

02 / Research progress

From a broad hope to a question we can answer

BACKGROUND · Why investigate this?

Diet is part of everyday life, which makes it a compelling research direction. But “Does diet help?” leaves several questions hidden. Help with pain? With overall disease activity? With future changes in bone? A useful review needs to say which of these a study actually addressed. It must also distinguish a plausible biological idea from a benefit demonstrated in people.

There is also a biological question to explore. The supplement study discussed in today's lesson asked whether a product intended to influence gut microbes could change selected immune measurements and disease activity. That is a testable research idea. It does not establish that diet causes SpA, or that changing the gut will repair the spine.

We therefore narrowed the immediate goal. Before drawing conclusions about treatment, we will map direct studies in people with axial SpA and make their comparisons clear. We will examine whole dietary programmes separately from supplements and products intended to influence gut microbes. Evidence from other diseases may suggest ideas, but it cannot stand in for results in axial SpA.

METHODS · What did we actually do today?

We wrote P003, our dietary-intervention review plan (summarized below). It asks the same practical questions of every study: who took part, what changed for them, what the comparison group received, what was measured, and what could otherwise explain the result. If dietary advice also includes exercise advice or more frequent staff contact, we will preserve that detail: it affects what we can attribute to diet itself.

To prepare the search, we saved 95 literature records from Europe PMC, a database of biomedical publications, and four selected study records from ClinicalTrials.gov, a registry where researchers describe planned and ongoing trials. The 95 records are search matches, not 95 relevant trials. We have not yet checked all of them against our inclusion rules. The four registry entries were chosen as starting points; they are not a complete inventory.

We then inspected a small selection of papers and registry entries to test whether our plan could handle real reporting problems. This was preparation for the review, not its full execution. We had already seen these examples when finalizing the plan, and that prior knowledge is recorded in the protocol. An Astra Ultra agent helped design the approach, an Astra medium agent checked sources, and a separate Astra high agent challenged the wording and interpretation. These are internal AI checks, not review by an external scientist.

RESULTS · What did those checks reveal?

1. Two reports can describe the same study differently

The full paper Mediterranean diet in axial spondyloarthritis: an observational study in an Italian monocentric cohort describes an observational comparison. In that kind of study, we cannot assume that chance determined who received the intervention. A likely related conference abstract describes the allocation as randomized—meaning assigned by chance. Chance assignment helps reduce systematic differences between groups at the start. Without it, the groups may differ for reasons that also affect the outcome. The conflicting accounts therefore change how confidently we can attribute differences to the dietary programme.

We are provisionally following the fuller methods in the paper while keeping the conflict visible. A published correction changes wording about age and body mass index; it does not resolve the allocation question. Some reported numbers also need reconciliation before we use them to estimate a treatment effect. The useful finding today is a reason for caution about interpretation, not a verdict on the Mediterranean diet.

Source: Ometto and colleagues (2021), Mediterranean diet in axial spondyloarthritis: an observational study in an Italian monocentric cohort. Full article inspected, with limits on supplementary material. See also the conference abstract POS0956 and correction.

2. Getting better is not the same as doing better than the comparison group

In Supplementation of omega-3 fatty acids in patients with ankylosing spondylitis, 24 people were assigned to a higher or lower omega-3 dose, and 18 completed the study. The abstract reports a statistically significant improvement in the higher-dose group's disease-activity score, the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI). Read on its own, that sounds encouraging.

But the treatment question is comparative: did the higher-dose group improve more than the lower-dose group? The abstract reports no statistically significant difference between the groups. We therefore cannot turn the change within one group into evidence that the higher dose was better. Equally, this result does not prove that the doses are equivalent or that omega-3 can never help. Both groups received omega-3, and we inspected only the abstract.

Source: Sundström and colleagues (2006), Supplementation of omega-3 fatty acids in patients with ankylosing spondylitis. Abstract only; PubMed ID 17062435. The doses were 4.55 and 1.95 grams per day, with follow-up to 21 weeks. These describe the experiment, not a dose recommendation.

3. A familiar disease score does not tell us who was enrolled

A third paper, Probiotic therapy for the treatment of spondyloarthritis: a randomized controlled trial, studied people with active SpA. Its abstract allows eligibility based on peripheral-joint activity—that is, joints outside the spine. It does not establish that everyone had axial SpA or that results for an axial subgroup can be separated. Before treating this as direct evidence for our question, we need the fuller eligibility details. That uncertainty is a reason to investigate, not to discard a result we dislike.

Source: Jenks and colleagues (2010), Probiotic therapy for the treatment of spondyloarthritis: a randomized controlled trial. Abstract only; PubMed ID 20716665. The abstract reports 63 participants and no significant benefit over placebo in the assessed core domains.
CONCLUSIONS · What follows from this?

We now have a plan that explicitly handles these problems. The next step is to complete the planned searches, check which records are relevant, link papers that report the same participants, and identify where full text is missing. Only then can we decide whether the evidence supports a meaningful comparison of interventions or mainly an account of unanswered questions.

Today's work supports that next step. It does not yet answer whether a particular diet improves axial SpA, and it provides no new evidence of reversal of established bone changes. No combined estimate across studies was calculated.

03 / Daily SpA lesson

Three measurements, three different questions

Suppose a treatment changes a measurement in the immune system. That can be an interesting result. But a patient may reasonably ask a different question: did people feel or function better? And for a disease that can change bone structure, there is another question again: did those structural changes slow down or reverse?

These questions belong together in our research, but the answer to one cannot simply supply the answer to the others. Today's synbiotic example makes the distinction concrete. A synbiotic is a type of supplement intended to influence gut microbes. The study tested one such product for 12 weeks, randomly assigning 48 people with axial SpA to the product or placebo. Thirty-eight completed follow-up.

The researchers measured selected immune cells and the activity of genes involved in immune signalling. They also assessed disease activity using two named scores, BASDAI and ASDAS-CRP. According to the abstract, some immune measurements differed significantly between the groups, while the disease-activity scores did not show a significant improvement with the supplement. Those findings can coexist: they concern different measurements.

What should we conclude? The immune findings justify more precise questions about the product and the measurements. They do not demonstrate that patients benefited. At the same time, a nonsignificant clinical result is not proof of no possible effect: we would need to inspect the size and uncertainty of the differences, how missing observations were handled, and the full methods before making a stronger judgment. Our access today was limited to the abstract.

There is one more detail worth keeping. Another paper reports regulatory T-cell measurements using samples from this same trial. It adds information about those participants, but it is not an independent experiment repeating the finding in a new group. Our review will link these papers rather than count them as two separate confirmations.

Main study: Ahangari Maleki and colleagues, Randomized double-blinded controlled trial on the effect of synbiotic supplementation on IL-17/IL-23 pathway and disease activity in patients with axial spondyloarthritis (PubMed ID 35947039). Companion: Khabbazi and colleagues, Effects of synbiotic supplementation on regulatory T cells' response in patients with axial spondyloarthritis: a randomized double-masked placebo-controlled trial (PubMed ID 36409223). Both inspected at abstract level.
The exact measurements, for when you want the technical detail

The between-group immune differences reported in the main abstract concern the proportion of IL-17-expressing CD4+ T cells and expression of the IL-17 and IL-23 genes. The finding does not apply to every immune assay in the study. BASDAI stands for Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index. The paper describes ASDAS-CRP as the ASAS-endorsed disease activity score using C-reactive protein. These are disease-activity measures, not measures of bone repair.

A question to carry into the next paper: What exactly changed, compared with what—and is that the outcome I care about?

That question lets us remain interested in a promising biological result without asking it to prove more than the experiment measured.

Check your understanding: does a changed immune marker with no significant clinical improvement prove that the supplement works—or that it cannot work?

Neither. It supports a limited statement about the measured immune response. The clinical result still needs its effect size, uncertainty and study limitations explained. Neither measurement, by itself, demonstrates repair of the spine.

Methods note

What this edition is based on

Today's completed task was to prepare P003, a plan for a review of dietary interventions in adults with axial spondyloarthritis. The plan separates whole diets from supplements and microbial products, distinguishes randomized comparisons from observational studies, and asks how symptoms, disease activity and structural outcomes were measured. Papers reporting the same participants will be linked before conclusions are drawn.

We saved 95 records from a targeted Europe PMC discovery search covering literature dates through 3 October 2026. This initial query included study-design terms such as trial and cohort. It is narrower than the broader search planned for the review and cannot establish complete coverage. None of the 95 records has yet undergone the planned systematic screening process. The four ClinicalTrials.gov records were deliberately selected examples rather than the result of a complete registry search.

The Mediterranean-diet paper and its correction were inspected as full text, with incomplete inspection of supplements and table pages. The omega-3, probiotic and synbiotic trial reports were inspected at abstract level. Registry data came from saved official API records; the Mediterranean protocol was inspected as PDF text. Earlier registry versions have not yet been reconstructed. No treatment effects were pooled, and no comprehensive correction or retraction search was completed for the abstract-only reports.

Internal AI review checked the plan and the limited claims used here. A separate editorial check assessed whether the clearer narrative preserved their meaning. Neither establishes external scientific validation. This is a dated research-progress report and educational explanation, not a completed systematic review or personal medical advice.
