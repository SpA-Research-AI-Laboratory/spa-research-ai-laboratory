02 / Research progress

What does a search result really represent?

Background: a plausible question needs a complete enough evidence trail.

“Could changing food help?” sounds like one question. In the literature it becomes many: a whole dietary pattern, a particular food, a supplement, a probiotic, or a programme combining several changes. A survey of what people eat answers a different question from a trial that assigns them to a specified diet. Our map keeps these categories separate so that evidence for one does not quietly become evidence for another.

Methods: cast a broad net, then inspect what came back.

We used the PubMed population and dietary terms frozen in our protocol, without restricting the search to randomized trials or favorable outcomes. We saved the returned identifiers and fetched every record. We then began with the first 50 records in the saved publication-date search order. The remaining 920 are explicitly pending; the first batch is not a representative sample from which to estimate the whole literature.

For ClinicalTrials.gov, we ran 33 combinations: three disease terms, each paired with 11 diet or supplement terms. After removing repeated registry identifiers, there were 55 records to inspect. Broad indexed searches also return unrelated conditions and drug studies. Each record therefore needed its eligibility, actual intervention and comparator read—not just its title. Other planned work, including additional registry coverage, complete citation tracing and historical registration checks, remains unfinished.

Results: useful leads, still unanswered questions.

One lead is a Portuguese low-starch study, NCT04386538, which registers a randomized comparison with general healthy-eating advice. This is a concrete candidate to trace. The record currently reports an unknown recruitment status and contains no posted results. That does not tell us whether the investigators completed the work or published elsewhere.

Original registry title: Effect of a Low Starch Diet in Patients With Ankylosing Spondylitis. ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04386538, current record retrieved 4 October 2026; last update posted 25 January 2022. Access: full current registry fields, not a historical-version audit or an outcome paper.

Another lead, axSEND, NCT06460805, illustrates why the comparison matters. It registers a partial enteral nutrition diet—a mixture of liquid formula and selected solid foods—in people with axial SpA and healthy volunteers. Both groups receive the diet. Healthy volunteers can help investigate biological responses, but they do not show what would have happened to comparable patients with axial SpA who did not receive the diet. Its eligibility also starts at age 16, so adult-only evidence would need to be separated for our review.

Original registry title: axSEND: Exploring Immune and Microbiota Effects of a Partial Enteral Nutrition Diet in Axial Spondyloarthritis. Access: current ClinicalTrials.gov record retrieved 4 October 2026. Registered methods are not proof of actual conduct or treatment benefit.

A linked publication can still be the wrong study.

A Mediterranean-diet registration, NCT07170384, labels a linked paper as a results reference. But the paper is the older Italian observational study by Ometto and colleagues, while this registration describes a different study in Turkey. We have therefore not counted that link as results of the registered trial. The label is a lead to check, not sufficient evidence of a match.

Ometto et al., Mediterranean diet in axial spondyloarthritis: an observational study in an Italian monocentric cohort (2021). The previously inspected full report and today's registry fields support the identity distinction. This is a linkage problem, not an allegation about anyone's conduct.

Conclusion: the evidence map is advancing; an efficacy verdict would be premature.

We also identified Sezgin and Yuksel's Mechanisms and clinical evidence of dietary patterns in axial spondyloarthritis, published on 14 July 2026. Its abstract reports searches through 1 June 2026. We inspected the abstract and accessible reference list, not the subscription full text. It is an overlap warning and a source of leads; we cannot yet say whether it already contains every feature of the map we plan to build.

We have not established whether a particular diet helps, whether no diet helps, or whether there are enough suitable studies for the planned comparison. Those conclusions require the unfinished screening, study linkage and methods checks. A current registration without posted results is also not proof that results are absent from the published literature.

03 / Today's SpA lesson

How do you hide a dietary change?

Imagine being asked to remove a food from your meals. You would usually know that your diet had changed. If your symptoms then improved, the observation would matter—but the cause could still be uncertain. The food, the extra attention, other changes in your routine and your expectations might all have changed together. A useful experiment tries to separate these possibilities.

The GlutenSpA protocol offers an unusually concrete example. Both groups were to receive advice on a gluten-free background diet. One group would then receive gluten-free bread, pasta and rice-flour capsules; the other would receive similar-looking gluten-containing products and gluten capsules. The foods were selected for similar appearance and taste, and repackaged neutrally, to help keep participants and staff unaware of the assignment.

The main planned comparison was change in the ASAS Health Index (ASAS-HI) at 16 weeks—a patient-reported measure of functioning and health. This is a question about how people feel and function, not a direct measurement of repair in spinal bone. The protocol also included other clinical measures and a smaller gut-microbiota substudy, examining bacteria in stool samples.

Notice what this design can and cannot isolate. It brings the comparison closer to gluten exposure while trying to share the surrounding dietary changes and attention. It still needs evidence that participants followed the assigned plan and that masking worked. Similar-looking foods are not automatically identical in every nutritional property. Results would also need to be interpreted in the population actually studied; the protocol excluded people with coeliac disease.

Source: Couderc et al., GlutenSpA trial: protocol for a randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trial of the impact of a gluten-free diet on quality of life in patients with axial spondyloarthritis, BMJ Open, 2020;10:e038715. DOI: 10.1136/bmjopen-2020-038715. Access: open full-text protocol, particularly Interventions, Eligibility criteria, Outcome measures and Blinding. Linked registration: NCT04274374. We are explaining the plan, not reporting a positive or negative trial result.

A question to carry away

If both groups improved equally, would that prove that removing gluten caused the improvement?

No. The intended test is the difference between the assigned groups. Improvement shared by both could have several explanations. Even a small or statistically nonsignificant between-group difference would need its uncertainty examined before concluding that the effects were equivalent.

The broader lesson is useful well beyond gluten: before asking “Did the diet work?”, ask “What exactly differed between the groups, and what outcome did the study measure?”
